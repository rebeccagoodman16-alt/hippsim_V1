Theta Resonance in CA1 Pyramidal Neurons

Rebecca Goodman · computational neuroscience lab · reduced two-compartment model and a 571-compartment reconstruction

Question What is the minimum biophysical complexity needed to reproduce CA1 filtering, resonance, phase locking and bursting?
Structured abstract

Abstract

Background. CA1 pyramidal neurons receive theta (4–12 Hz) and gamma (30–100 Hz) rhythmic input, and recordings show they respond preferentially to theta frequencies through hyperpolarization-activated (Ih) and M-type (IM) potassium currents. Recent models (Lowet et al. 2023; Ceballos et al. 2025) link these intrinsic currents to distinct burst and single-spike firing modes.

Aim. To determine which features of CA1 frequency preference can be reproduced by progressively simpler models, adding one mechanism at a time.

Methods. A two-compartment model (soma + dendrite; leak, axial coupling, dendritic Ih from Lowet et al. 2023, optional somatic IM/INaP from Golomb et al. 2006), and a 571-compartment model on a reconstructed CA1 morphology (Jarsky et al. 2005) with an Ih density gradient. Impedance was computed from linearized (quasi-active) theory and checked against simulation; spike timing was quantified with pairwise phase consistency.

Results. The passive model is strictly low-pass. Dendritic Ih produced a resonance whose strength depended on conductance (Q 1.02 → 1.53 for ḡh 0.05 → 2 µS) and whose frequency depended on kinetics: 2.3 Hz with Lowet's τh (0.6–3 s), 8.9 Hz with τh = 40 ms. Freezing the Ih gate removed the peak but kept the attenuation, separating kinetic from conductance effects. Somatic IM + INaP produced a 5 Hz resonance at −60 mV (confirmed by simulation within 0.3%) and almost none near rest, matching Hu et al. (2002). In the reconstructed cell, input resistance was 66 MΩ at the soma and local resonance rose from ~5 Hz at the soma to ~8–9 Hz in parts of the tuft.

Conclusion. Theta-range subthreshold resonance requires a slow restoring current with the right time constant; dendritic Ih with Lowet's kinetics is too slow to provide it alone, and somatic IM near threshold is the most parsimonious addition. Reproducing burst-versus-single-spike preferences will require an intrinsic burst mechanism not present in these models.

Where things stand

Five findings so far

Q = 1.0 at gh = 0.05 µS

The earlier Ih runs used 1/40 of Lowet's conductance. At that level the model is purely low-pass, so 8 Hz beat 40 Hz by filtering alone.

2.3 Hz Q = 1.53

With Lowet's own Ih (ḡh = 2 µS, τh 0.57–2.9 s between −50 and −70 mV), a peak appears at delta, not theta.

8.9 Hz τh = 40 ms

The same ḡh with a 40 ms time constant peaks in theta with the same Q. Conductance sets the strength of the peak; τh sets its frequency.

4.10 vs 4.14 MΩ at 8 Hz

Freezing h at rest keeps the same 8 Hz attenuation but removes the peak (Q = 1.0). The amplitude drop comes from added conductance; the resonance comes from Ih kinetics.

2–5 Hz real CA1, 33 °C

Hu et al. (2002) measured 2–5 Hz resonance in CA1 slices at 33 °C and ~7 Hz at 38 °C. The 2.3 Hz peak is in the slice range; speeding τh 3× (≈ body temperature) moves it to 3.9 Hz.

Implication. In Lowet's HHca2 model, theta preference likely does not come from dendritic Ih alone. The leading candidate is somatic IM (τz = 75 ms) together with INaP, the Golomb/Hu perithreshold theta mechanism. That is the next justified addition.
