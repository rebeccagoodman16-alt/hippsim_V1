The cell, in plain language

What it isA CA1 pyramidal neuron is the main output cell of the hippocampus. It has a cell body (soma) and a long branching apical dendrite that collects input.
What it hearsCA3 cells talk to the middle of the dendrite (Schaffer collaterals). Entorhinal cortex talks to the far tip. Inputs arrive in rhythms: theta (~4–12 Hz) during exploration, gamma (~30–100 Hz) nested inside it.
What it doesMany CA1 cells are place cells: they fire when an animal is in one spot. They fire either single spikes or short bursts, and Lowet et al. (2023) found bursts lock to theta while single spikes also lock to gamma.
Why rhythms matterIf a cell responds more strongly to some input frequencies than others, it acts like a tuned receiver. That tuning could decide which messages get passed on, and when.

Two words this page uses a lot. A filter passes slow signals and weakens fast ones, like a thick wall muffling high notes. Every membrane does this. Resonance is different: the cell responds best to one middle frequency, like a swing that goes highest when pushed at its natural rhythm. Resonance needs a current that pushes back on slow changes, such as Ih or IM. Telling these two apart is the main job of this project.

IntroDoes a bigger input current make a bigger voltage response?Yes. In a linear membrane, doubling the current doubles the voltage.

Example from the model

Sine current injected into the dendrite at 2 Hz (passive model):

Current amplitudeDendrite voltage amplitudeSoma voltage amplitude
50 pA0.53 mV0.44 mV
100 pA1.06 mV0.88 mV
200 pA2.12 mV1.76 mV
400 pA4.23 mV3.53 mV

Every row has the same ratio: 1.06 mV per 100 pA = 10.6 MΩ. That ratio is the impedance.

Think of it this way

  • Current amplitude (pA): how much charge you push in per unit time.
  • Voltage amplitude (mV): how far the membrane potential swings in response.
  • Frequency (Hz): how many times per second the swing repeats.
  • Impedance (MΩ): how many mV you get per nA at that frequency.

Connection to the CA1 model

Turn the amplitude up far enough and the soma reaches spike threshold (−50 mV, 20 mV above rest). Whether it gets there depends on both amplitude and frequency; see "How much current does it take to fire?"

Important distinction

Raising the amplitude makes the input stronger; it does not change how efficiently the membrane responds. That efficiency is impedance, and in a linear model it stays the same at every amplitude. Once active channels start to open or close, this stops being true; see "When does doubling stop doubling?"

IntroWhat is impedance, in one line?Voltage response divided by current input, measured at one frequency: Z(f) = V(f) / I(f).

Example from the model

Same model, measured at the dendrite:

Frequency|Zdd|Voltage for a 100 pA input
2 Hz (delta)10.6 MΩ1.06 mV
8 Hz (theta)7.8 MΩ0.78 mV
40 Hz (gamma)2.5 MΩ0.25 mV

Think of it this way

Resistance (Ohm's law, V = IR) for signals that change over time. MΩ × nA = mV, so a 10 MΩ impedance turns 0.1 nA into 1 mV.

Connection to the CA1 model

Impedance is the main quantity in this project: a curve of |Z| against frequency shows which input rhythms the cell favours. Every result in the Paper is a statement about the shape of that curve.

Important distinction

Input impedance (inject and record at the same place, Zdd) vs transfer impedance (inject at the dendrite, record at the soma, Zsd). The transfer impedance shows what actually reaches the spike-generating soma.

IntroWhy do faster inputs give smaller voltage changes?The membrane is a capacitor. It needs time to charge, and fast inputs reverse before it has finished.

Example from the model

With C = 1 nF and gL = 0.05 µS, the membrane time constant is τm = C/gL = 20 ms. The corner frequency is roughly 1/(2πτm) ≈ 8 Hz. Above that, |Z| falls steadily: 10.6 MΩ at 2 Hz, 2.5 MΩ at 40 Hz.

Think of it this way

Filling a bucket that has a small hole in it. Pour slowly and the level rises a long way. Switch between pouring and scooping 40 times a second and the level barely moves.

Connection to the CA1 model

This is why a passive CA1 model responds more to theta than to gamma, with no special channels at all. It is a low-pass filter: the response always falls with frequency and never peaks.

Important distinction

Preferring theta over gamma is not resonance. Resonance means the response peaks at a non-zero frequency, so the cell responds less to slower inputs as well. A passive membrane cannot do that.

Paper 3.1 · Explorer: "passive" preset

IntermediateWhat is resonance, and how is it measured?A peak in |Z| at a preferred frequency. Its strength is Q = |Z(peak)| / |Z(lowest frequency)|.

Example from the model

Q = 1 means there is no peak (pure low-pass). In this project, Q = 1.53 with dendritic Ih (ḡh = 2 µS, peak at 2.3 Hz).

Think of it this way

A swing pushed in time with its natural rhythm. Pushes that are too slow and pushes that are too fast both move it less.

Connection to the CA1 model

For a peak you need a slow restoring current, one that opposes voltage changes but only after a delay. Slow inputs give it time to cancel them; fast inputs are filtered by the capacitor. The middle frequency wins. In CA1, Ih and IM play this role.

Important distinction

Report Q and fres together. A peak at 0.6 Hz with Q = 1.02 is technically resonance but means little biologically.

Paper 3.2 · Lab lesson 3

IntroWhy is the soma's voltage smaller than the dendrite's?Current leaks out through the dendrite's membrane before it reaches the soma, and more leaks at high frequencies.

Example from the model

FrequencyDendrite (Zdd)Soma (Zsd)Fraction reaching soma
2 Hz10.6 MΩ8.8 MΩ0.83
40 Hz2.5 MΩ1.6 MΩ0.64

In the reconstructed cell, from a site 300 µm out, 26% of an 8 Hz signal reaches the soma, but only 8% of a 40 Hz signal.

Think of it this way

A leaky garden hose: the longer the hose and the more holes it has, the less water arrives at the end.

Connection to the CA1 model

The axial conductance gax sets how strongly the two compartments are coupled (steady-state fraction = gax/(gax+gL) = 0.833). With two compartments there is only one "distance"; the 571-compartment model has hundreds.

Important distinction

Attenuation is a second filter stacked on the first, so distal gamma input is doubly disadvantaged by the time it reaches the soma.

Lab lesson 4 · Paper 3.6

IntermediateWhat does Ih do: conductance or kinetics?Its conductance lowers the response at every frequency; its slowness (τh) is what creates the peak and sets where it falls.

Example from the model

Dendrite model2 Hz8 Hz40 HzShape
Passive10.67.82.5low-pass
Ih 2 µS, Lowet kinetics (τh ≈ 0.6–3 s)5.65.02.3tiny peak ≈ 2 Hz
Same, gate frozen5.65.02.3low-pass, no peak
Ih 2 µS, τh = 40 ms3.34.72.3peak ≈ 9 Hz (theta)

|Zdd| in MΩ.

Think of it this way

A thermostat. How hard the heater can push is the conductance; how long the thermostat takes to react is τh. A slow thermostat corrects only slow drifts, so the frequencies it lets through are set by its delay.

Connection to the CA1 model

This is the project's main reduced-model finding: with the published Lowet kinetics, dendritic Ih resonates in the delta range, not theta. The kinetics, and the temperature they were measured at, matter as much as the density.

Important distinction

The frozen-gate control keeps the conductance but stops the gate from moving. If the peak disappears and the attenuation stays, the peak came from the kinetics and the attenuation from the conductance.

Paper 3.2 · Explorer: compare presets

IntermediateWhy does resonance depend on membrane potential?IM and INaP only switch on near spike threshold, so their resonance appears when the cell is depolarized.

Example from the model

Somatic IM + INaP: almost no peak at −80 or −70 mV, but a 5 Hz peak at −60 mV (theory 31.4 MΩ, simulation 31.4 MΩ). Hu et al. (2002) called these "H-resonance" (hyperpolarized) and "M-resonance" (near threshold).

Think of it this way

Two thermostats with different settings: Ih works in the cold range (hyperpolarized) and IM in the warm range (depolarized). INaP is an amplifier that boosts the response near threshold.

Connection to the CA1 model

During exploration, CA1 cells are depolarized by input, so the near-threshold M-resonance may be the one that shapes spiking. That is why somatic IM is the next rung of the model.

Important distinction

Impedance is always measured at a membrane potential. Quote the holding voltage with every resonance value.

Paper 3.5

AdvancedWhen does doubling the current stop doubling the voltage?When the input is big enough to open or close channels. The response then grows faster than the input (amplification) or slower (saturation).

Example from the model

Dendritic sine at 2 Hz, with Ih + somatic IM + INaP (no spikes):

CurrentSoma voltage amplitudemV per 100 pA
50 pA0.34 mV0.68
100 pA0.68 mV0.68
200 pA1.44 mV0.72
400 pA13.2 mV3.3 (!)

Up to 200 pA the cell behaves linearly. At 400 pA the swings reach the voltages where INaP turns on, and the response jumps almost fivefold.

Think of it this way

A microphone: quiet speech is recorded faithfully, while shouting distorts. Impedance describes the "quiet" regime.

Connection to the CA1 model

All impedance curves in this project use small inputs (20 pA) and the quasi-active method: each channel is treated as a fixed resistor plus a slow "spring" around rest. That is accurate for small signals and is why theory and simulation agree to within 0.3%. Spiking, bursting and phase locking are large-signal behaviours, so they are measured by simulation.

Important distinction

"Impedance stays the same" is a property of the small-signal regime, not of the cell. In real CA1 cells, synaptic input often pushes the cell out of that regime.

Paper 2.7 · Paper 2.5

IntroHow much current does it take to make the cell fire?Enough to swing the soma 20 mV, from rest (−70 mV) to threshold (−50 mV). Divide 20 mV by the transfer impedance to estimate it.

Example from the model

Dendritic sinePredicted (20 mV ÷ |Zsd|)Simulated
2 Hz20 / 8.8 MΩ ≈ 2.3 nA2.0 nA: 0 spikes · 2.4 nA: 6 spikes · 2.8 nA: 18 spikes
40 Hz20 / 1.6 MΩ ≈ 12.4 nAno spikes at 2.8 nA

Think of it this way

Throwing a ball over a fence. Height reached = throw strength × efficiency. Amplitude is the throw strength, impedance is the efficiency, and the fence height is threshold minus rest.

Connection to the CA1 model

This card ties the others together: amplitude × impedance = voltage, and voltage against threshold decides whether a spike occurs. A cell's frequency preference below threshold therefore becomes a preference in its spiking.

Important distinction

The prediction holds because the passive model stays linear up to threshold. With INaP, the cell fires at lower currents than the linear estimate (see the previous card).

Paper 3.4 · Lab lesson 5

IntermediateWhat is phase locking, and what does PPC measure?Phase locking means spikes fall at a consistent point in each input cycle; PPC measures how consistent, from 0 (random) to 1 (identical phase).

Example from the model

Reconstructed cell, 0.3 nA somatic sine at 8 Hz: about 5 spikes per cycle at a consistent phase (vector strength R = 0.77; 0.93 after adding IM). At 40 Hz, the same amplitude produces no spikes.

Think of it this way

Clapping along to music. Clapping on the beat every time is high PPC; clapping at random is PPC ≈ 0.

Connection to the CA1 model

Lowet et al. (2023) used PPC = (N R² − 1)/(N − 1), which corrects R for the number of spikes N (Vinck 2010). CA1 place cells phase-lock to theta, and their firing phase carries information about position.

Important distinction

R is biased upward when there are few spikes; PPC is not. Use PPC when comparing conditions with different spike counts.

Paper 3.4 · API: ppc()

IntermediateWhat counts as a burst, and why does it matter?A burst is two or more spikes closer together than 14.6 ms (Lowet) or 14 ms (Ceballos); everything else is a single spike.

Example from the model

Lowet et al. and Ceballos et al. found that bursts and single spikes prefer different input rhythms: bursts favour theta, single spikes follow faster rhythms.

Think of it this way

Two channels on one radio, with bursts carrying one message and single spikes another, multiplexed in the same cell.

Connection to the CA1 model

The models here have no intrinsic burst mechanism (no Ca2+ currents or burst-generating INaP/IM dynamics), so this is hypothesis H4: not yet testable. It is the next major step.

Important distinction

Spikes that happen to fall close together are not a burst mechanism. A burst is defined by ISI, but explaining one requires a current that drives it.

The input-resistance mismatch matters less than it looks. Scaling the area scales every conductance and capacitance together, so time constants, resonance frequency and Q stay the same; only the mV-per-nA scale changes.

The questions, plainly

What we are testing, and why

StagePlain questionWhy it matters biologicallyAnswer so far
1 · PassiveIf the cell were just a leaky bag of membrane, how would it handle slow vs fast input?Sets the baseline. Anything a passive cell already does needs no special channel to explain it.It only muffles fast input. No preferred frequency.
2 · IhDoes adding HCN channels to the dendrite give the cell a favourite frequency?HCN channels are dense in CA1 dendrites and are blamed for theta resonance.Yes, but Lowet's slow kinetics put it at ~2 Hz. Speed of the channel decides where the peak sits.
2 · controlIs the change from the channel's timing, or just from extra open channels?Separates "the cell is leakier" from "the cell is tuned".Timing makes the peak; extra conductance makes the overall drop.
3 · Rhythms + noiseDoes theta get through better than gamma, and does background noise change that?Real inputs are noisy rhythms, not clean waves.Theta gets through better by filtering. Noise level tested was too small to matter.
4 · SpikesDoes the cell fire at a consistent point in each cycle, and can it burst?Spike timing relative to theta is how place cells code position (phase precession).It locks to the rhythm but cannot burst.
Next · IMDoes a slow potassium current in the soma give theta tuning near firing threshold?Hu et al. found this "M-resonance" at −60 mV; Golomb showed IM drives bursting.Theory says yes: ~5 Hz at −60 mV. Simulation check pending.
